*My intern project at Rothschild & Co / Redburn Atlantic, summer 2026. The firm's note, model and deck use Capital IQ data and Redburn templates, so I have kept those off the site. What follows is the argument in my own words, and a chart I have built here from stated figures rather than from any firm exhibit.*

Long Garmin. Its two biggest lines are Fitness and Outdoor, and together they are about two-thirds of group revenue. Fitness is the one the whole debate turns on, so the equity question reduces to a single number: how fast Fitness compounds from here.

Consensus has it at roughly **7-8% a year [E]**. My work put it closer to **14% [E]**, with Garmin taking a large share of that growth.

<figure class="figure"><img src="/pitches/img/grmn/fitness.png" alt="Garmin Fitness revenue: history and two forward scenarios" loading="lazy"/><figcaption>The debate in one picture. Fitness has already gone from about 1.3bn in 2020 to about 3.0bn FY26e; the question is which line it follows next. [E], illustrative.</figcaption></figure>

Two things drive the higher number. Younger buyers are under-penetrated and increasingly treat self-optimisation as something worth signalling, so the pool of buyers is widening rather than saturating. And the product lead in GPS accuracy and battery life is built up over years and hard to copy, so as the pool widens Garmin should keep most of the new demand rather than ceding it.

The part I actually did the work on was the evidence. Rather than assume a "niche" market has to mature, I used search-trend data to test whether category growth, and Garmin's share of it, were speeding up or slowing down, and then reconciled that line by line against consensus.

It is a clean example of the way I like to work: a qualitative case about accuracy, battery and brand, disciplined by an alternative-data test of the one number the whole thing depends on. If Fitness compounds at 14% rather than 7-8%, the current multiple is wrong; if it does not, I am.
