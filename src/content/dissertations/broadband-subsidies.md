abstract: >
  The UK's Superfast Broadband programme was meant to close the digital divide.
  This dissertation asks what it actually did to network quality over fifteen
  years. I use the programme's staggered rollout and correct for the fact that
  treated areas were not chosen at random, matching them with genetic matching
  and then estimating a heterogeneity-robust difference-in-differences. Measured
  against independent physical speed-test data, the programme did push superfast
  coverage to nearly everywhere. But it also left a "copper trap": in the areas
  it treated, the faster full-fibre technology arrived more slowly than in areas
  it left alone.

The faculty gave this its prize for the best dissertation in econometrics that year. I still call it "the copper trap" after the thing it found.

The method is a heterogeneity-robust difference-in-differences on the staggered BDUK rollout, with genetic matching to build a credible control group for areas that were not chosen at random, and physical speed-test data rather than self-reported coverage as the outcome. I check it the ways you are supposed to: event-study pre-trends, a Goodman-Bacon decomposition, placebo-in-time tests, and a Monte Carlo exercise.

The finding is two-sided. The programme worked on its own terms and got superfast coverage close to universal. But the same subsidy design seems to have slowed the next technology down: full fibre came later to treated areas than to untreated ones. A conceptual model, a social planner's first-best against a subgame-perfect second-best, gives the reason why a subsidy that locks in "good enough" copper-based superfast can crowd out the generation after it.
