Evaluation of UK Broadband Subsidies

Abstract. The UK's Superfast Broadband programme was a major infrastructure initiative designed to eliminate the digital divide. This dissertation evaluates its aggregate impact on network quality over a 15-year horizon. Exploiting the programme's staggered rollout and correcting for endogenous treatment selection via genetic matching, I apply a heterogeneity-robust difference-in-differences estimator. Using independent physical speed-test data, I find the intervention successfully expanded superfast coverage to near-universal levels — but also find evidence of a longer-term "copper trap": a policy-induced reduction in the deployment of faster full-fibre technology relative to untreated regions.

Method. A heterogeneity-robust difference-in-differences design on the staggered BDUK rollout, with genetic matching to correct for the endogenous selection of treated areas, evaluated against independent physical speed-test data rather than self-reported coverage. Robustness includes event-study pre-trend checks, a Goodman-Bacon decomposition, placebo-in-time tests and a Monte Carlo exercise.

Headline findings. The programme drove superfast coverage to near-universal levels, but the same subsidy design appears to have slowed the subsequent rollout of full fibre in treated areas — the “copper trap.” A conceptual model (social planner’s first-best vs. a subgame-perfect second-best) motivates why a subsidy that locks in “good-enough” copper-based superfast can crowd out the next technology generation.
